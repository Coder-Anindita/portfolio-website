Picture this: it's 2 AM, your exam is in seven hours, and your physics textbook is a 600-page PDF. You don't want to *read* it. You want to **ask it questions**, like texting a friend who has read the whole thing.

"What's Newton's second law?"
"Explain the derivation on page 142 like I'm five."
"Give me 3 practice questions from chapter 4."

That's a simple LLM-powered app. Building one is how I ended up understanding **LangChain**, so let's build it together (on paper) and see why LangChain exists.

![LangChain chains together a PDF, chunks, vectors and an LLM](/images/blog/langchain-cover.svg)

## So… what *is* LangChain?

The textbook definition:

> **LangChain is an open-source framework for building applications powered by Large Language Models (LLMs).**

My version: an LLM on its own is a brilliant brain in a jar. It can't read your files, it can't search a database, and it forgets you the moment the chat ends. LangChain is the **body, hands and nervous system** you build around that brain.

### Which languages can I use it with?

| Language | Support |
| --- | --- |
| 🐍 Python | ✅ Official, and the most popular |
| 🟨 JavaScript / TypeScript | ✅ Official (LangChain.js) |
| ☕ Java | ⚠️ Not official, but **LangChain4j** is a solid community project |
| 🐘 PHP | ⚠️ Community ports only |

If you're starting out, use **Python**. Most tutorials, docs and Stack Overflow answers assume it.

## Why do we even need a framework?

You need a framework when something has lots of moving parts, so let's design our app and count them.

Meet **PDFBuddy** 📚: you upload a PDF, ask a question, and get an answer based on that PDF.

![High-level design of a chat-with-your-PDF app](/images/blog/langchain-pdf-app-simple.svg "The 10,000-ft view of PDFBuddy")

Here's the flow:

1. The **user asks a question.**
2. A **semantic search** runs over the PDF (stored in a database) and pulls out the **relevant pages only**.
3. We build a **system query** = relevant pages + the user's question.
4. That goes to the **Brain** 🧠.
5. The Brain writes the **final answer**.

### What does "the Brain" need to be good at?

Two things:

- **Natural Language Understanding (NLU):** it has to *get* what you mean, even with typos, slang or vague phrasing. "wat is f=ma thing" should still work.
- **Context-aware text generation:** it has to write an answer **based on the pages we gave it**, not on whatever it half-remembers from the internet.

If you're thinking "that's just an LLM", you're right. Hold that thought.

## "Why not just send the whole PDF to the LLM?"

Fair question. Why bother with a semantic search step? Why not send all 600 pages and let the LLM figure it out?

Think about how you ask your math teacher for help:

![Meme: dropping the whole textbook vs pointing at page 142](/images/blog/langchain-meme-math-teacher.svg "We all know which student gets help faster.")

You don't hand over the whole book and say "somewhere in here I'm confused." You say *"Page 142, question 3, this step."* Your teacher reads one paragraph and answers in two minutes.

LLMs work the same way. Here's why semantic search wins:

- 💸 **Cost:** LLM APIs charge per token. Sending 600 pages per question means paying to process 600 pages every time someone asks something.
- 📏 **Context limits:** every model has a maximum input size (the *context window*). Large documents, or ten of them, may simply not fit.
- 🎯 **Accuracy:** with too much text, models can lose the important detail. Less noise gives sharper answers.
- ⚡ **Speed:** less input means faster responses.
- 📈 **Scale:** semantic search works the same for 1 PDF or 10,000 PDFs. "Send everything" doesn't.

## Semantic search, explained with cricket and biryani 🏏🍛

Old-school **keyword search** matches words. Ask "Who is a great cricketer?" and it looks for the literal words *great* and *cricketer*.

Now suppose your document says *"Virat is a batting legend."* There's no keyword match, so keyword search comes back empty.

**Semantic search** matches **meaning**. It knows "batting legend" and "great cricketer" are about the same idea. Here's how.

### Step 1: Turn text into numbers (embeddings)

An **embedding model** reads a piece of text and turns it into a list of numbers, called a **vector**, that represents its *meaning*.

```text
"Virat is a batting legend"     →  [0.90, 0.90, 0.00]
"Messi's stunning free kick"    →  [0.90, 0.10, 0.00]
"Biryani recipe"                →  [0.00, 0.00, 0.95]
"Who is a great cricketer?"     →  [0.80, 0.85, 0.05]
```

### Step 2: What's a vector? What are dimensions?

A **vector** is just an arrow pointing somewhere in space, written as a list of numbers.

Each number is a **dimension**. In my toy example there are 3 dimensions, and you can pretend they mean something like:

- dimension 1 → how "sports-y" is this?
- dimension 2 → how "cricket-y" is this?
- dimension 3 → how "food-y" is this?

Real embedding models use far more dimensions: 384, 768, 1536, sometimes 3072. Those dimensions don't have neat human labels. They're patterns the model learned from huge amounts of text. The idea is the same, though: **every number captures some aspect of meaning.**

### Step 3: How are they placed in space?

Each vector is a point in that space, and **similar meanings end up close together**. Cricket sentences cluster together, food sentences cluster somewhere else, and tech sentences are off in their own corner.

![Animated: the query lands near cricket sentences and gets the highest similarity](/images/blog/langchain-embedding-space.svg "Watch the query land right next to Virat 🏏")

### Step 4: What is "similarity" and how do we measure it?

Similarity is how close two meanings are. The most common way to measure it is **cosine similarity**, which compares the *angle* between two arrows:

- arrows point the same way → angle ≈ 0° → similarity ≈ **1** (same meaning)
- arrows are perpendicular → similarity ≈ **0** (unrelated)

The formula looks scarier than it is:

```text
cosine_similarity(A, B) = (A · B) / (|A| × |B|)
```

That's "multiply matching numbers and add them up, then divide by the lengths of both arrows." Plugging in our toy vectors:

| Chunk | Similarity to "Who is a great cricketer?" |
| --- | --- |
| 🏏 Virat is a batting legend | **0.99** 🥇 |
| ⚽ Messi's stunning free kick | 0.76 (it's sports, but not cricket) |
| 🍛 Biryani recipe | 0.04 (not even close) |

The Virat chunk wins, and **that** is what goes to the LLM. Not the whole PDF, just the best-matching pieces.

<Callout type="tip" title="Mental model">
  Embeddings are like GPS coordinates for meaning. Semantic search is asking "what's nearest to me?"
</Callout>

## The full picture

Here's the detailed architecture of PDFBuddy:

![Detailed RAG architecture: ingestion, query, and answer flows](/images/blog/langchain-rag-architecture.svg "Data flows left to right, then back to the user")

**① Ingestion** happens once, when the PDF is uploaded:

1. **PDF upload:** the user drops a file in.
2. **AWS S3:** we store the raw file safely in the cloud.
3. **Document loader:** extracts plain text from the PDF.
4. **Text splitter:** cuts the text into small, overlapping **chunks** (say, about one paragraph each).
5. **Embedding model:** turns every chunk into a vector.
6. **Vector database:** stores all those vectors so they're fast to search.

**② Query** happens every time someone asks a question:

7. **User query** comes in.
8. The query goes through the **same embedding model**. This matters: you can't compare GPS coordinates with postcodes.
9. **Semantic search** finds the closest chunks in the vector DB.

**③ Answer:**

10. **System query** = top chunks + the user's question.
11. **The Brain (LLM)** reads it and writes an answer.
12. **Final answer** goes back to the user, based on *their* PDF.

<Callout type="info">
  This pattern has a name: **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)**. Retrieve the right context, then let the LLM generate an answer from it.
</Callout>

That's the design. Now let's look at what makes it hard to build.

## Challenge #1: The Brain is HEAVY 🏋️

We need an LLM. There are three options:

- **Build your own:** needs a research lab, mountains of data and millions of dollars. Next.
- **Open-source models** (Llama, Mistral, etc.): free to download, but you have to host them yourself.
- **Paid APIs** (OpenAI, Google Gemini, Anthropic Claude): someone else hosts them.

So what does "heavy" mean? An LLM is basically billions of numbers (parameters). A 70-billion-parameter model at 16-bit precision is about **140 GB of weights**, and those have to sit in GPU memory just to answer one question. That means several expensive data-center GPUs, plus the electricity, cooling and engineers to keep them running.

![Meme: laptop on fire vs calling an LLM API](/images/blog/langchain-meme-laptop.svg "RIP to every laptop that tried.")

That's why companies like OpenAI and Google host these models on their own servers and give you an **API**. You send text and get text back. They handle the GPUs. Problem #1 solved.

## Challenge #2: Orchestrating all the moving parts 🎻

Count the components in our diagram: **loader, splitter, embedding model, vector DB, retriever, LLM**, plus all the code that connects them. Every one of these comes from a different vendor with a different SDK.

Here's what usually happens. You write the whole app using Gemini's SDK, it works, and you're happy. Then:

![Meme: switching LLM provider without and with LangChain](/images/blog/langchain-meme-switch-provider.svg "Based on a true story. Probably yours.")

Without a framework, switching providers means rewriting code, because every SDK is shaped differently:

```python title="without_langchain.py"
# Gemini version
from google import genai
client = genai.Client()
reply = client.models.generate_content(model="gemini-2.5-flash", contents=question)
answer = reply.text

# OpenAI version: different import, different call, different response shape
from openai import OpenAI
client = OpenAI()
reply = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="gpt-4o-mini",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}],
)
answer = reply.choices[0].message.content
```

Now multiply that by every component in your pipeline. 😵

## Enter LangChain: plug and play 🔌

LangChain gives every component a **common interface**. A loader is a loader and an LLM is an LLM, whoever made them. To swap one, you change **one line**:

```python title="with_langchain.py" {3}
from langchain.chat_models import init_chat_model

llm = init_chat_model("openai:gpt-4o-mini")   # was "google_genai:gemini-2.5-flash"
answer = llm.invoke(question).content
```

![LangChain components as swappable building blocks](/images/blog/langchain-plug-and-play.svg)

And the whole ingestion pipeline from our architecture fits in a few lines:

```python title="ingest.py"
from langchain_community.document_loaders import PyPDFLoader
from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter
from langchain_openai import OpenAIEmbeddings
from langchain_community.vectorstores import FAISS

docs = PyPDFLoader("physics.pdf").load()                      # loader
chunks = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(
    chunk_size=1000, chunk_overlap=200
).split_documents(docs)                                       # splitter
db = FAISS.from_documents(chunks, OpenAIEmbeddings())         # embeddings + vector DB

relevant = db.similarity_search("What is Newton's second law?", k=3)  # semantic search
```

Want Chroma instead of FAISS? Hugging Face embeddings instead of OpenAI? Change the import and that line.

## LangChain's other superpowers ⚡

**1. Chains: pipelines where output becomes input automatically.** This is where the name comes from. Join steps with `|` and data flows through them:

```python
from langchain_core.prompts import ChatPromptTemplate
from langchain_core.output_parsers import StrOutputParser

prompt = ChatPromptTemplate.from_template(
    "Answer using only this context:\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"
)
chain = prompt | llm | StrOutputParser()

chain.invoke({"context": relevant, "question": "What is Newton's second law?"})
```

Prompt → LLM → clean string, with no glue code. You can build long, branching workflows the same way.

**2. Model-agnostic development.** Write your app once and pick the best or cheapest model later. You aren't locked into one vendor.

**3. A complete ecosystem.** Hundreds of integrations: loaders for PDFs, websites, Notion and CSVs; many splitting strategies; most major vector databases; and the big LLM providers.

**4. Memory and state handling.** LLMs are stateless and forget everything between calls. LangChain gives you tools to keep conversation history, so when the user asks "explain *that* again", the app knows what "that" is.

## What can you build with it? 🛠️

- 💬 **Conversational chatbots** that remember the conversation
- 🤖 **AI agents** that decide which tools to call (search, calculator, APIs) to finish a task
- 📚 **AI knowledge assistants**, like PDFBuddy for your company's internal docs
- ⚙️ **Workflow automation:** read emails, pull out key details, update a sheet, draft a reply
- 📝 **Summarization and research helpers:** turn a 50-page report into a 5-bullet brief

## Is LangChain the only option?

No. Two popular alternatives:

| Framework | Best known for |
| --- | --- |
| 🦙 **LlamaIndex** | Data-heavy apps: indexing and retrieval over your documents |
| 🌾 **Haystack** | Production-ready search and question-answering pipelines |

All three can build PDFBuddy. LangChain is usually the go-to for its huge ecosystem and its flexibility across agents, chains and RAG.

## TL;DR 🧾

- **LangChain** is an open-source framework for building LLM-powered apps (Python and JS officially).
- A real LLM app has many moving parts: **loader → splitter → embeddings → vector DB → retriever → LLM**.
- **Semantic search** sends only the relevant chunks to the LLM. That's cheaper, faster and more accurate, like asking your teacher about one page instead of the whole book.
- **Embeddings** are vectors that capture meaning; **cosine similarity** measures how close two meanings are.
- LangChain makes every component **plug and play**, so switching providers is a one-line change, and **chains** connect it all into a pipeline.

Next time someone says "I built a chat-with-your-PDF app," you'll know what's going on behind it. 😎
